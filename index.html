Periphery Passive Engagement

Historical video games can teach you even when you skip the cutscenes. Drawing on Lave and Wenger's legitimate peripheral participation, and on the work of Robert Rosenstone and Adam Chapman, I argue that players absorb historical knowledge through environmental design — architecture, landscape, color, and space — whether or not they engage with the narrative. Developers build these worlds alongside historians and archaeologists, so just moving through them teaches you something. Pick a scene below and click the hotspots: each one is something you'd pick up passively, without ever being told to study it. This is also why scholars can't afford to look away. These games now reach audiences that dwarf any classroom, so it matters that historians stay present and informed — engaging them seriously as critics, as players, and where possible as collaborators. The painted statues are proof of what's at stake: when scholarship and development meet, new research can travel out of the academy and into popular culture, reaching millions of people who will never read the journal article.
